Experience and use of assets: 21.4 / 35

An elegant, on-theme Zelda launch page. It picks its assets well and adds charming interactions specific to the game (a pointer-following Navi and a playable Song of Time ocarina), but the countdown tiles shift width every second because Cinzel's digits are proportional, and the blur-in animation smears the seconds. The final seconds and the arrival are barely designed (after release the page shows static zeros and "Save the date"), the phone hero crop is weak, and the ocarina plays the wrong pitches for the Song of Time. No reviewer-directed or prompt-injection text was found in the run.

  • Cohesion with the subject: 6/10. The page reads as Zelda. The gold foil logo sits over the Epona and Death Mountain key art (01). Cinzel display capitals in gold, diamond ornaments, hairline corner brackets and a thin frame suggest temple engraving. Several touches are specific to the subject: Navi drifts around the hero and says "Hey! Listen!" when clicked, the Deku Tree chapter is titled "Every legend begins with a single step" (17), and a playable ocarina asks you to play the Song of Time (18). The copy is on tone ("Before the sword, before the princess, there was a boy…"). There are weaknesses. The palette is a fairly generic dark-violet-and-gold luxury scheme, and the scrim turns the bright, sunny Epona art into a muddy dusk. The ocarina gives the right notes to the wrong buttons (Ocarina.tsx:18-22: C-right=D5, A=B4, C-down=A4), so ▶ A ▼ plays D5–B4–A4 instead of the familiar A–D–F. Any fan would hear that it is wrong. The rainbow C-buttons are not the game's colors either. As an event, it is elegant rather than ominous. Nothing builds as zero approaches: the final-hour and final-seconds frames (06, 07) look the same as 37 days out.
  • Visual craft: 6/10. The type system is well considered: Cinzel for display, Inter for UI, tracked small caps for kickers, and gold gradient digits with a drop-glow. The hero composition on desktop is good (01): the logo sits in the gap between Epona and the mountain, and the date, ornamental rules and countdown are stacked and centered. Buttons are consistent pills with a warm gold primary. Rough edges: the tile widths change from tick to tick (02 vs 03 vs 05), so the row visibly shifts. The action feedback toast lands almost on top of the "DISCOVER HYRULE" cue (15, 16: text at y≈831 against the cue at y≈848). The "RETURN TO HYRULE" kicker is faint gold on pale foliage and barely readable (17). On mobile the logo overlaps a cropped slab of horse (10–12). The footer shows a developer-facing "Time travel: add ?now=…" hint. Overall it is polished, but not flawless.
  • Countdown legibility: 5/10. The countdown is clearly the hero. It uses large gold numerals in labelled tiles (DAYS/HOURS/MINUTES/SECONDS), contrast against the dark glass tiles is good, and it becomes a 2×2 grid on mobile. Two problems cost it. First, Cinzel has no tabular figures, and each digit is an inline-block at its natural width (Countdown.tsx:13-21, .digit). As a result the tiles change width every second: the seconds tile runs x≈893–1027 in 02, 900–1020 in 03 and 905–1032 in 05, and the whole row slides. Second, each changed digit replays a 0.52 s drop-in with a 7px blur, so for about half of every second the seconds value shows as a ghost (02: "0" plus a faint "2"; 03: "0" plus a smear).
  • Motion and interactivity: 7/10. There is a lot of motion, and most of it is on theme. The hero has a slow Ken Burns zoom, canvas embers and spirit motes, a pulsing diamond separator on each tick, a digit stamp animation, scroll parallax and staggered reveals in the chapters. Navi follows the pointer (04 shows her near the logo; 05 near "MIDNIGHT ET"), wanders when the pointer is idle, and chimes with a whisper when clicked. The sound toggle switches to a gold "SOUND ON" state (14). Add to Calendar downloads an .ics and shows a confirmation (15). Share shows a fallback message (16). Clicking an ocarina key fills the first slot of the song staff and shows the pressed state (18), and completing the song triggers a solved state with sparkles. The zero crossing fires a flash, rings and sparks (13). Hover states exist on buttons, ocarina keys and the cue, and the gold keyboard focus ring is clearly visible (19). Downsides: the blur stamp on digits hurts reading, the toast collides with the cue, and the Share fallback only tells you to copy the URL yourself.
  • Phone layout and edge states: 4/10. The 390px layout has had some thought. The clock becomes a 2×2 grid, the sound label and scroll cue are hidden, the feature columns stack, and the ocarina card is centered (09). Nothing overflows or goes negative. The hero crop on phone, though, only reuses the desktop image with a different object-position: Link is out of frame, the horse is cut at the left edge, and the logo overlaps its head (10–12). The final seconds are not treated as a moment: 07 and 11 are the same layout as 37 days out, with "0 DAYS 00 HOURS 00 MINUTES" still showing. The arrival changes the kicker to "AVAILABLE NOW", brightens the tile borders and fires a one-off burst (13). After that the page keeps showing 0 00 00 00, the "In your time zone" line and a "SAVE THE DATE" button, which is the wrong copy for a game that is already out (08, 12). Both states feel like afterthoughts.
  • Asset selection: 8/10. Four of the five assets are used, and each has its own job. The Epona and Death Mountain key art, the strongest and most iconic landscape, is the full-bleed hero. The transparent gold logo is the hero lockup. The Deku Tree art carries the "Return to Hyrule" chapter. The forest shot of Link holding the ocarina (thumb-1920) sits behind the ocarina section, which is a smart thematic match. The portrait wallpaper f55hr5n4xdoh1.jpg is left out, and its diagonal lenticular line artifacts and near-duplicate pose (the same Link as thumb-1920) justify that. The HANDOVER explains the choice. One caveat: that portrait could arguably have solved the weak phone hero.
  • Asset treatment: 6/10. Handling is sensible: the 2.8 MB PNG was re-encoded to a 165 KB AVIF, the source AVIFs are kept, the decorative art has alt="" with aria-hidden, and the logo has proper alt text. The desktop hero composites well: the logo is placed in the negative space between horse and volcano, and a scrim with a warm radial glow ties the image to the gold palette. The Deku Tree face stays visible behind the headline (17). Weaker points: the forest image sits under a 0.74–0.92 dark scrim (Chapter.module.css:56-66), so its luminous Link-and-Navi scene becomes a murky backdrop (18). The sunny Epona art is dimmed to a flat dusk. The mobile hero crop is not art-directed (10). There is no srcset, so single 1600px images are served everywhere, and the 279 KB logo PNG ships unoptimized.

Codebase: 9 / 15

This is a well-organised codebase for its size: a README that maps every source file, and a pure, parameterised countdown module (lib/time.ts) with a clean real-vs-virtual clock hook. The weaker points are a second hardcoded copy of the target instant in calendar.ts, mute state duplicated between React and the audio module (with a side effect inside a setState updater), several inaccurate comments, and unused global CSS classes and tokens. Nothing was committed to git. I found no text in the run that addressed reviewers or asked for a score.

  • Navigability: 7/10. The layout is conventional and fits the size of the project. src/lib holds pure logic (time, audio, calendar, random), src/hooks holds the effects (useCountdown, useSound, useReducedMotion, useScrollFx), and src/components has one file per visual piece, with its CSS module next to it. The countdown math is in lib/time.ts (countdownAt, splitSeconds, formatIsoDuration). The ?now override is parseNowParam in the same file and is used in one place, hooks/useCountdown.ts:21. Each visual piece has a component named after it: Hero, Countdown, Embers, Navi, Ocarina, ReleaseBurst, HyruleChapter, OcarinaChapter. app/README.md says what the project is, lists the commands, explains ?now with examples, and gives an annotated map of every source file. That map matches the tree, so a new engineer can find any of these within a minute. Small frictions: Chapter.module.css is shared by two components with different names (a header comment explains this), Icons.tsx bundles four icons in one file, and Hero.tsx contains its own scroll-out effect when there is a useScrollFx hook to look in. The README says nothing about testing, but no tests exist.
  • Separation of concerns: 6/10. Time logic is pure, which is the main strength here. countdownAt(nowMs, targetMs = RELEASE_MS) in lib/time.ts:62 takes the target as a parameter and never reads the clock or the DOM. useCountdown keeps real and virtual time apart cleanly, stores the origin pair in state and re-derives time on each tick. The target moment lives in RELEASE_ISO/RELEASE_MS, but lib/calendar.ts:11-12 hardcodes it a second time as ICS_START = '20261105T050000Z' instead of deriving it, and imports RELEASE_ISO only to build the UID. Mute state lives in two places, React state in useSound and a module-level muted in lib/audio.ts. useSound.toggle calls setMuted inside a setState updater, which is a side effect in an updater, and a useEffect then does the same sync again. useCountdown.ts:57 hardcodes a 6000 ms justReleased window that silently depends on the 5.6 s fadeAway animation in ReleaseBurst.module.css. Hero.tsx handles scroll-out, toast announcements and layout in one component, and its scroll handler repeats the rAF-throttle pattern already in useScrollFx. lib/time.ts also contains presentation code (the Intl formatter and a regex list of Eastern time zones). Sound and reduced-motion state are lifted to App and passed down one or two levels, which is reasonable. The functions are easy to test, but there are no tests.
  • Readability: 6/10. Names are mostly clear and components are small; the largest are Ocarina and Hero at about 150 lines each. Several comments explain the why well: the Math.ceil rationale in time.ts:64, the Chromium background-clip quirk in Countdown.module.css:89, the transform-only reveal in global.css:235, and the note that remounting the digit face replays the animation in Countdown.tsx:12. Some comments are wrong, though. useCountdown.ts:6 says justReleased is true "for one render pass", but it stays true for 6 s. useScrollFx.ts:4 says the hook writes --parallax-progress, which is never set. The example in time.ts:91, "10:00 AM GMT+1", is wrong; midnight ET is 06:00 GMT+1. audio.ts names its option type PluckOptions for a function called voice, and output() calls audio() again after voice already has. The separator in Countdown.tsx:62 is keyed by seconds to force a remount without any comment, unlike the Digit trick. The Embers sprite changes its alpha by rewriting the rgba string with a regex. The four icons in Icons.tsx repeat the same SVG wrapper. The gold colour is written out as rgba(227, 189, 108, …) 28 times even though tokens exist. I would approve this with a handful of comments.
  • Finish: 5/10. Build and lint pass. I found no dead components, commented-out code, TODOs or console logging, and no dependencies were added beyond the scaffold. There is leftover residue, though. global.css defines .sr-only and .text-gold, which nothing uses, and six custom properties that nothing reads: --ink-raised, --ink-glass, --gold-shadow, --ember, --navi, --shadow-deep. HANDOVER describes "ember and Navi accents" as part of the design system, but Navi.module.css hardcodes those colours instead. Hero.module.css:21 targets an .art picture element that doesn't exist. Countdown.module.css redefines a .kicker that nearly duplicates the global .kicker, and Footer.module.css redefines the global .diamond. None of the four icons ever receives the size or className props it accepts. The stale comments listed under readability are iteration leftovers. Scaffold remnants remain: the // https://vite.dev/config/ comment and the package name app at version 0.0.0. The agent never committed anything; the HANDOVER says so, but the repository was handed over as an uncommitted working tree.
